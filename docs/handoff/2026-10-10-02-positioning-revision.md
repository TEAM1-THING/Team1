### Positioning

> **Second Chair is a live strategic copilot for financial advisors.** It listens
> to the conversation, understands the client's questions, objections and
> priorities, and privately tells the advisor what matters and what to say
> next, grounded in the advisor's prepared materials and client data, and
> labelled so the advisor can tell a fact from a calculation from AI-generated
> strategy.

The shift from 01: not "it answers client questions" but "it recognises the
decision-relevant moment and proposes the next move". The advisor wants more
than a correct answer; they want the one that moves the conversation forward.

**It listens for these moments, not only questions:** question, objection,
hesitation, contradiction, misunderstanding, buying signal, disclosure,
emotional concern, change in priorities, commitment, unresolved concern.

**Three layers:**

1. **Intelligence.** Name the moment and what is underneath it. Client: "locking
   this much into the portfolio worries me, what if I need cash?" becomes:
   objection = liquidity; underlying concern = access and optionality; relevant
   prepared material = the cash-reserve analysis; relevant client fact = cash on
   hand; advisor's objective = the plan they prepared.
2. **Tactical response.** Not just retrieval ("the plan leaves $180K liquid") but
   a recommended move with a suggested phrasing and a short *why this response*:
   acknowledge the concern first, separate required liquidity from idle cash,
   anchor to the prepared figure, keep an off-ramp.
3. **Guardrail and provenance.** Every figure and claim on the card is labelled:
   - **PREPARED FACT**: comes from the advisor's own materials (cited).
   - **CALCULATED**: computed here; math and assumptions shown.
   - **TACTICAL SUGGESTION**: framing and sequencing generated by Second Chair.
   - **DO NOT CLAIM**: what the evidence does not support (e.g. that the
     prepared figure is sufficient for every emergency scenario).

The card also keeps **conversation state**: which concerns have been raised,
which are resolved, which are still open. When the client raises a new objection,
the recommended move visibly changes. That change is the demo.

**How to word it.** We found no advisor product whose core proposition is live
next-move coaching; Jiffy.ai's in-meeting prompts are the closest. That is an
absence from a quick search, not proof. On stage say "we did not find one", not
"nobody does this". The defensible differentiator is the combination: live
tactical assistance, wealth-advisor context, and explicit provenance. Zocks also
covers in-person meetings, so in-person alone is not our edge.

### Guardrails (recommended; **needs a team decision**)

These are not yet in `CLAUDE.md` because the team has not agreed them.

1. **Objective.** The aim is the client's stated goals and an informed decision,
   not conversion. Rephrase "maximize the probability of a successful outcome" as
   "respond to what the client actually said, consistent with the plan the
   advisor prepared". Advisers owe fiduciary duty and broker-dealers owe a
   best-interest standard (Reg BI); a tool that coaches pure persuasion invites
   supervision and record-keeping questions. Design constraint, not legal advice.
2. **Disclosed, not covert.** Cluely's pitch is that it is undetectable. In a
   regulated meeting that is a liability, and California requires all-party
   consent to record. The "invisible analyst beside you" image is a strong demo
   hook; the product itself should be disclosed to the client. This is also the
   clearest contrast with Cluely.
3. **No pressure tactics.** No urgency, scarcity or fear framing in suggestions.
4. **Suggestions obey the same rules as facts:** provenance labels, the number-trace
   check, Mandate bounds, and *defer* when a question is outside the Mandate.
5. **Every card is a record.** Suggestions can be reviewed after the fact; the
   after-meeting record should include what was suggested and what the advisor said.

### Demo scenes (illustrative figures; use a fictional client file)

1. **Liquidity.** Client: "I'm worried that locking this much in means I won't
   have enough access to cash." The card names the objection, shows the cash on
   hand and the prepared liquid figure, and suggests separating required
   liquidity from idle cash, with an off-ramp to start smaller.
2. **Wait six months.** Client: "The market seems too uncertain, I'll wait."
   The card returns to the stated long horizon, surfaces the phased-entry
   structure already in the plan, and suggests asking whether the hesitation is
   fear of a temporary loss or a need for the cash.
3. **Strategy shift.** The client then raises a *new* concern. The recommended
   move visibly changes and the open-concerns list updates.

**Description (read to testers word for word). Primary:**

> Second Chair is an unfair advantage for wealth advisors sitting across from a client. It is not a note-taker: it works during the meeting, not after. Beforehand, the advisor gives it their plan and client file. **Fallback if the agent or testers balk at "unfair advantage":**

> Second Chair is a live copilot for wealth advisors sitting across from a client. It is not a note-taker: it works during the meeting, not after. Beforehand, the advisor gives it their plan and client file. Then, as the client talks, it listens for the moments that matter, like a worry hiding behind a question, a contradiction, or a buying signal, and privately shows the advisor what's going on and what to say next, using the advisor's own numbers. Each suggestion is marked as a prepared fact, a quick calculation, or just a suggested way to put it, so the advisor always knows what's solid.

**Why this wording.**
- It opens with the feeling (unfair advantage, whispering in your ear) and names
  what it is not, in the first two sentences, because testers hear it once over
  the phone.
- "Cheating" is not in the description on purpose; it is put to testers as a
  question (below), so their reaction is data instead of a reaction to our word.
- It does **not** say whether the client is told. That is the riskiest open
  choice, so it is tested in the interview instead of assumed.
- No jargon (no "RAG", "provenance", "Mandate"): the three labels are described
  in plain words.

**Questions for the interviewer.** Testers are "finance professionals", not
necessarily advisors, so open with a role question. Question 2 checks the
"is it a note-taker?" confusion directly.

1. What do you do, and how often do you sit down with clients, in person or on a call?
2. In your own words, what does this do? *(diagnostic: a note-taker answer means the wording failed)*
3. *(priority)* Tell me about a moment in a client meeting where you froze, got caught off guard, or said something you wish you hadn't. What happened?
4. *(priority)* Some people would call a tool like this an unfair advantage. Others might call it cheating. Where do you land, and how would your clients react?
5. *(priority)* If it privately suggested what to say next, what would make you trust a suggestion, and what would make you ignore it?
6. *(priority)* Where is the line between helpful coaching and pushing a client?
7. *(priority)* Would you tell the client it was in use? Who else, such as compliance, would need to be comfortable? What would stop you from using it?
8. Tell me about the last time someone asked something you couldn't answer on the spot. What did you do?

What we want to learn: whether testers describe it as a live copilot (not a
note-taker); whether the "hard moment in the room" pain is real and in what
words; whether advisors accept live phrasing suggestions; where they draw the
line between coaching and cheating or pushing; how they feel about disclosing it.

### Revised build order (cut from the bottom)

1. Prep ingestion: deck, documents and a fictional **client file**; the 30-second
   Mandate summary the advisor confirms.
2. **Moment detector and card** on a typed or replayed transcript: classify the
   moment, retrieve the relevant prepared facts and client facts, generate moment,
   concern, recommended move, suggested phrasing and *why*.
3. Provenance labels, the number-trace check, Mandate bounds, defer and
   DO NOT CLAIM.
4. **Conversation state**: raised, resolved and open concerns; the card updates
   when a new objection arrives. This is the demo's centre.
5. Bright Data: pre-flight check for stale numbers in the deck; public-fact lookups
   with citations.
6. Live speech with the in-person / online toggle (replay mode already exists
   from step 2 and stays as the fallback).
7. After-meeting record and follow-up list, including what was suggested.
8. Stretch: the live disclosure-coverage checklist from 01.

Steps 1 to 4 need no speech at all, so the core demo works on a replayed
meeting. Live speech stays late on purpose.
